A website does not need a complete redesign every time it stops pulling its weight.

Sometimes it needs a content audit: a focused check for outdated details, fuzzy service descriptions, missing customer answers, broken paths to contact, and pages that no longer match how the business actually works.

This is not a keyword-stuffing exercise. It is a practical way to make the site easier for a potential customer to understand and easier for your team to use as a reliable source of truth.

Google's guidance on [helpful, reliable, people-first content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) is a useful standard here. The goal is content that helps a visitor complete a real task—not copy written mainly to manipulate a ranking system.

Set a timer for an hour. Open the homepage, top service pages, contact page, and a few recent blog posts. Then work through this checklist.

## First 10 minutes: can a stranger explain what you do?

Start on the homepage without reading it like the person who wrote it.

Ask a simpler question: if someone landed here from a search result, a recommendation, or a link in an email, could they explain the business after one quick scan?

They should be able to find:

- What you offer.
- Who it is for.
- Where or how you work.
- What makes an inquiry a good fit.
- The next sensible action to take.

Marketing language is not automatically bad. But phrases such as “solutions,” “elevate your business,” or “a better experience” become a problem when they replace the actual service. A visitor should not have to decode whether you build websites, repair appliances, handle bookkeeping, offer consulting, or sell a product.

Try this test: highlight the first headline, paragraph, and call to action on the page. Do those three pieces tell the same clear story? If not, rewrite the opening before adding more sections below it.

## Next 10 minutes: check the facts customers rely on

Outdated business details create more damage than a slightly stale design. They can send customers to the wrong place, create missed calls, start awkward sales conversations, and make a business look less dependable.

Check the basics across the site:

- Phone number and email address.
- Service area, office address, or remote availability.
- Hours and response-time expectations.
- Current services and packages.
- Pricing language, estimates, deposits, or consultation policies.
- Team, credentials, partner, and software references.
- Links to booking, social, review, portfolio, or client portals.

Pay special attention to pages that are easy to forget: the footer, contact page, older blog posts, thank-you messages, PDFs, and downloadable guides.

If a detail needs explanation, do not hide it behind a vague promise. For example, “fast response” is less useful than “we reply to new project inquiries within one business day.” Only make commitments the business can consistently keep.

## Minutes 20–30: audit the service pages for decision-making information

A good service page does more than announce a service exists. It helps the right person decide whether to contact you.

For each important service page, look for answers to these questions:

1. What problem does this service solve?
2. Who is it for—and who is it not for?
3. What is included in a typical engagement?
4. What does the customer need to provide or prepare?
5. What happens after they reach out?
6. Which related service or resource should they consider next?

You do not need to publish every operational detail. You do need enough context for a visitor to make a good next move.

That is especially important for service businesses. A page that only says “custom solutions” invites a lot of unqualified conversations. A page that names the work, describes the fit, and explains the process helps both customers and the people answering inquiries.

If the same question keeps coming up on sales calls, in emails, or in the contact form, it is a strong candidate for a service-page section or a short FAQ. Real customer questions are often better content prompts than a generic editorial calendar.

## Minutes 30–40: follow every important path to contact

Now act like a motivated visitor. From the homepage and each major service page, try to take the next step.

Can you easily find a route to call, email, book, request a quote, or submit an inquiry? Is the call to action specific about what will happen?

Weak calls to action often say:

- Learn more.
- Get started.
- Contact us.
- Submit.

Those can work in context, but they do not carry much information by themselves. A clearer action might say:

- Request a website project estimate.
- Book a 20-minute discovery call.
- Ask about AI assistant setup.
- Send a maintenance support request.

Google's [link best practices](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/links-crawlable) recommend crawlable links and descriptive anchor text. That is good usability advice too: people should know where a link leads before they click it.

Check forms as part of this pass. The form should collect enough information to route the request well without turning the inquiry into homework. Tell people what happens after they submit it, when they can expect a reply, and what to do if their situation is urgent.

## Minutes 40–50: find the content that is saying nothing

Most sites have pages that technically exist but do not earn their place.

Common examples include:

- Service pages that repeat the same generic paragraph.
- Location pages with a city name swapped into identical copy.
- Blog posts that summarize a broad topic without answering a practical question.
- Old announcements that no longer apply.
- Image-heavy sections with little readable explanation.
- FAQs that avoid the questions customers actually ask.

Do not rush to delete everything short. First decide what each page should do.

A useful page should have a job: answer a question, explain a service, reduce uncertainty, show relevant work, prepare a lead, support a customer, or point to the next page. If it does none of those, combine it with a stronger page, rewrite it around a clear purpose, or retire it.

Google's [Search Essentials](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials) are another good reminder that search visibility starts with accessible, useful public content—not tricks. A page with a real job is usually easier to improve than a page assembled only because a site “needed more content.”

## Final 10 minutes: turn findings into a short action list

End the audit by sorting notes into three buckets.

### Fix now

These are items that create immediate confusion or lost opportunities:

- Wrong contact details.
- Broken links or forms.
- Old services, hours, or pricing claims.
- Missing contact paths on high-traffic pages.
- Misleading statements about availability or guarantees.

### Improve next

These are high-value clarity upgrades:

- Rewrite the homepage opening.
- Add service-fit and process details.
- Replace vague buttons with specific calls to action.
- Add answers to recurring customer questions.
- Improve form instructions and confirmation language.

### Build later

These are content opportunities that need some planning:

- A new service page for an established offer.
- A case study or portfolio story.
- A customer-question blog post.
- A comparison page that helps customers choose between options.
- A better resource or onboarding guide.

Keep the list small. Five completed fixes are more valuable than a spreadsheet of fifty “someday” ideas.

## What to do after the audit

A content audit is most useful when it becomes part of how the business operates. Review the site after a service change, a new offer, a process update, a recurring customer question, or a month of form submissions. The website should keep up with the business rather than becoming a time capsule.

For teams using AI tools, clear website content is also a safer input. A well-defined service page and a form with clear expectations give an assistant something reliable to summarize, route, or turn into a draft reply. Vague pages force people—and software—to guess.
